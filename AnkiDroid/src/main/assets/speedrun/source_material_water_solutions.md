## wa-01: Why water behaves unusually

Water is bent rather than linear, and oxygen pulls electron density away
from the two hydrogens, so the molecule carries a partial negative charge
near the oxygen and partial positives at the hydrogens. This polarity lets
each molecule hydrogen bond with several neighbours. Hydrogen bonding is
what gives water its unusually high boiling point, high heat capacity, and
high heat of vaporisation for a molecule of its size, and it is why ice —
held in an open lattice — is less dense than liquid water and floats.

## wa-02: Water as a solvent

Polar and ionic substances dissolve readily in water because water
molecules surround and stabilise the separated particles, a process called
solvation or hydration. Nonpolar substances do not dissolve well; rather
than water actively repelling them, the system simply gains more from
water hydrogen bonding with itself than with a nonpolar molecule. This is
the basis of the hydrophobic effect, which drives lipid bilayer formation
and protein folding.

## wa-03: Autoionisation and the pH scale

Water self-ionises slightly, so even pure water contains a small
concentration of hydrogen and hydroxide ions. At 25 degrees Celsius the
product of those two concentrations is a constant, 1.0 x 10^-14. pH is
defined as the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration, so
neutral water at that temperature has a pH of 7. Because the scale is
logarithmic, a change of one pH unit is a tenfold change in hydrogen ion
concentration.

## wa-04: Acids and bases

Under the Bronsted-Lowry definition an acid donates a proton and a base
accepts one. Every acid has a conjugate base, formed by losing that
proton, and every base has a conjugate acid. Strong acids and bases
dissociate essentially completely in water, while weak ones establish an
equilibrium in which most of the substance remains undissociated. The
strength of a weak acid is expressed by its dissociation constant, or by
the negative logarithm of that constant, pKa.

## wa-05: Buffers

A buffer resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are
added. It consists of a weak acid together with its conjugate base, which
can absorb added protons or added hydroxide respectively. A buffer works
best when the two components are present in comparable amounts, which
occurs when the pH is near the weak acid's pKa; the useful range is
roughly one pH unit either side of that value. Blood is buffered largely
by the carbonic acid and bicarbonate system.

## wa-06: Titration curves

Titrating a weak acid with a strong base produces a curve with a gently
sloping buffering region followed by a steep rise at the equivalence
point. At the half-equivalence point exactly half the acid has been
converted to its conjugate base, the two are equal in concentration, and
the pH equals the pKa — which is why that point is the standard way to
read a pKa off a curve. The equivalence point of a weak acid titration is
above pH 7, because the conjugate base left in solution is itself basic.

## wa-07: Solubility and concentration

Solubility describes how much solute can dissolve before a solution
becomes saturated. For sparingly soluble ionic compounds this is captured
by a solubility product constant. Adding an ion already present in the
equilibrium pushes it toward the solid and reduces solubility — the
common ion effect. Concentration itself can be expressed several ways,
including molarity (moles per litre of solution) and molality (moles per
kilogram of solvent); molality is preferred where temperature changes,
since volume expands but mass does not.

## wa-08: MCAT relevance and common traps

Recurring points include recognising that ice floating is a consequence of
hydrogen bonding geometry, that pH is logarithmic so small numerical
changes are large concentration changes, and that pH equals pKa at the
half-equivalence point rather than at the equivalence point — a very
common confusion. Another trap is assuming the equivalence point is always
pH 7; that holds only for a strong acid with a strong base. Students
should also be able to explain the hydrophobic effect without describing
water as "repelling" nonpolar molecules.
