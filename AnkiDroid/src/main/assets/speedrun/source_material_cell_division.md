as `source_material.md` — standard cell biology, original wording and
organisation, not copied from any copyrighted source. Maps to AAMC content
category **2C** (processes of cell division, differentiation, and
specialization); cards carry `topic::cell_division`. Every card must cite
a chunk ID (`cx-01` … `cx-08`).

## cx-01: The cell cycle at a glance

The cell cycle alternates between interphase, where the cell grows and
copies its DNA, and the mitotic phase, where it physically divides.
Interphase subdivides into G1 (growth and normal function), S (DNA
synthesis), and G2 (further growth and preparation for division). Most of
a cell's life is spent in interphase. Cells that stop dividing, such as
mature neurons, exit the cycle into a resting state called G0.

## cx-02: Checkpoints

Progression is controlled at checkpoints that verify conditions before
committing to the next stage. The G1 checkpoint asks whether the cell is
large enough and the DNA undamaged before entering S phase; the G2
checkpoint verifies that replication finished correctly; and a checkpoint
during mitosis confirms that chromosomes are properly attached to the
spindle before they are pulled apart. Failure of checkpoint control
allows damaged cells to keep dividing, which is central to how cancers
develop.

## cx-03: Mitosis produces identical cells

Mitosis divides the replicated genome into two identical nuclei. In
prophase chromosomes condense and the spindle forms; in metaphase they
align along the cell's midline; in anaphase sister chromatids are pulled
to opposite poles; and in telophase nuclear envelopes reform around the
two sets. Cytokinesis, the physical splitting of the cytoplasm, usually
overlaps with telophase. The result is two diploid cells genetically
identical to the parent.

## cx-04: Meiosis produces gametes

Meiosis takes one diploid cell through two consecutive divisions to yield
four haploid gametes. DNA is replicated only once, before the first
division, which is why the chromosome number halves. Meiosis I separates
homologous chromosome pairs and is therefore called the reductional
division; meiosis II separates sister chromatids and resembles mitosis
mechanically. A common exam trap is assuming both divisions halve the
chromosome number — only the first does.

## cx-05: Sources of genetic diversity

Two events in meiosis I generate most of the variation among gametes. In
prophase I, homologous chromosomes pair and exchange segments in crossing
over, producing chromosomes that mix maternal and paternal sequence. In
metaphase I, each homologous pair orients on the spindle independently of
the others, so which member of each pair goes to which pole is
effectively random. Together these produce enormous combinatorial
diversity before fertilisation adds more.

## cx-06: Nondisjunction

If chromosomes fail to separate properly — nondisjunction — the resulting
gametes carry the wrong number of chromosomes. Nondisjunction in meiosis
I means homologues failed to separate, so all four resulting gametes are
abnormal; in meiosis II sister chromatids failed to separate, so two are
abnormal and two are normal. Fertilisation involving such a gamete
produces an embryo with an incorrect chromosome count, which is the
mechanism behind several well-known chromosomal disorders.

## cx-07: Differentiation and potency

Differentiation is the process by which a cell becomes specialised for a
particular function. Crucially, it generally involves changes in which
genes are expressed, not loss of genetic material — most differentiated
cells retain the full genome. Cells are described by potency: totipotent
cells can form any cell type plus supporting tissues, pluripotent cells
can form any body cell type, and multipotent cells are limited to a
related family of types.

## cx-08: MCAT relevance and common traps

High-yield points include knowing that DNA replicates once but the cell
divides twice in meiosis, that crossing over occurs in prophase I rather
than in mitosis, and that mitosis yields two diploid cells while meiosis
yields four haploid ones. Another recurring trap is thinking
differentiation deletes unused genes; it regulates their expression
instead. Students should also be able to trace how a checkpoint failure
leads to uncontrolled division.
