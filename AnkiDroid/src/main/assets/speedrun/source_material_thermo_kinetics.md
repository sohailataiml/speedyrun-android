# Source material: chemical thermodynamics and kinetics

**Provenance note:** original content written for this project, same terms
as `source_material.md` — standard physical chemistry, original wording
and organisation, not copied from any copyrighted source. Maps to AAMC
content category **5E** (principles of chemical thermodynamics and
kinetics); cards carry `topic::thermo_kinetics`. Every card must cite a
chunk ID (`tk-01` … `tk-08`).

## tk-01: Thermodynamics versus kinetics

Thermodynamics asks whether a reaction is favourable and where the
equilibrium lies; kinetics asks how fast it gets there. The two are
independent, and conflating them is one of the most common errors on this
material. A reaction can be strongly favourable thermodynamically and still
be immeasurably slow — the conversion of diamond to graphite is favourable
but does not visibly occur — because favourability says nothing about the
height of the barrier in the way.

## tk-02: Enthalpy

Enthalpy change is the heat absorbed or released at constant pressure. A
negative value means heat is released and the reaction is exothermic; a
positive value means heat is absorbed and it is endothermic. Bond breaking
requires energy while bond forming releases it, so the sign of the overall
enthalpy change reflects whether the bonds formed in the products are
collectively stronger than those broken in the reactants.

## tk-03: Entropy

Entropy is a measure of how many microscopic arrangements are consistent
with a system's state — loosely, its disorder or dispersal of energy.
Entropy increases when a solid melts, when a liquid vaporises, when a
gas expands, and generally when the number of gas molecules rises during a
reaction. The second law states that the entropy of the universe increases
for any spontaneous process, which permits a local decrease in a system's
entropy provided the surroundings increase more.

## tk-04: Gibbs free energy

Gibbs free energy combines the two: the change in free energy equals the
change in enthalpy minus the absolute temperature times the change in
entropy. A negative free energy change means the process is spontaneous in
the direction written, a positive value means it is not, and zero means
the system is at equilibrium. Because temperature multiplies the entropy
term, reactions with opposing enthalpy and entropy signs switch between
spontaneous and non-spontaneous depending on temperature.

## tk-05: Free energy and equilibrium

The standard free energy change is related to the equilibrium constant: a
large equilibrium constant, meaning products are favoured, corresponds to
a negative standard free energy change, and a constant less than one
corresponds to a positive value. When the constant equals one the standard
free energy change is zero. This link is what allows a measured
equilibrium position to be translated into an energetic statement, and
vice versa.

## tk-06: Reaction rates and rate laws

The rate of a reaction expresses how quickly reactant concentrations fall
or product concentrations rise. A rate law relates rate to reactant
concentrations raised to experimentally determined exponents called
orders. Those orders must be measured; they cannot be read off the
balanced equation, which is a frequent misconception. The overall order is
the sum of the individual orders, and the rate constant itself varies
strongly with temperature.

## tk-07: Activation energy and catalysts

Every reaction must pass through a high-energy transition state, and the
energy required to reach it from the reactants is the activation energy.
Raising temperature speeds a reaction because a larger fraction of
molecules have enough energy to clear that barrier. A catalyst speeds a
reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation
energy; it is not consumed, it speeds the forward and reverse directions
equally, and — critically — it does not change the equilibrium position or
the free energy change. Enzymes are biological catalysts.

## tk-08: MCAT relevance and common traps

The single most tested idea here is that catalysts and enzymes change the
rate but never the equilibrium or the overall energetics. Other recurring
points include reading reaction coordinate diagrams to identify activation
energy versus overall energy change, recognising that spontaneous does not
mean fast, and knowing that reaction orders come from experiment rather
than stoichiometry. Students should also be able to predict how changing
temperature affects a reaction whose enthalpy and entropy terms oppose
each other.
