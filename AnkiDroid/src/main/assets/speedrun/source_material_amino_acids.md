## aa-01: The shared backbone of an amino acid

Every standard amino acid is built on the same skeleton: a central alpha
carbon bonded to four groups — an amino group, a carboxyl group, a
hydrogen, and a variable side chain conventionally called R. Because the
alpha carbon carries four different substituents in every amino acid
except glycine, it is a chiral centre, and the amino acids used in
proteins are the L-enantiomers. Glycine is the exception: its side chain
is a single hydrogen, so its alpha carbon has two identical substituents
and it is achiral.

## aa-02: Why side chains are the whole story

Since the backbone is identical across all twenty, everything that
distinguishes one amino acid from another lives in the side chain. Side
chains are usually grouped by how they behave in water: nonpolar
(hydrophobic), polar but uncharged, acidic (negatively charged at
physiological pH), and basic (positively charged at physiological pH).
This grouping predicts where a residue tends to sit in a folded protein —
hydrophobic side chains are typically buried away from water in the
interior, while charged and polar ones are typically exposed on the
surface.

## aa-03: Zwitterions and titration behaviour

At physiological pH an amino acid is not neutral in the naive sense; the
carboxyl group has lost a proton and the amino group has gained one, so
the molecule carries both a negative and a positive charge at once. This
doubly-charged but net-neutral form is called a zwitterion. Because there
are at least two ionisable groups, titrating an amino acid produces a
curve with multiple buffering regions, and the pH at which the molecule
carries no net charge is its isoelectric point, or pI.

## aa-04: The peptide bond

Amino acids are joined when the carboxyl group of one attacks the amino
group of the next, releasing a molecule of water — a condensation, or
dehydration, reaction. The resulting amide linkage is the peptide bond.
It has partial double-bond character because the nitrogen's lone pair
delocalises into the adjacent carbonyl, which makes the bond rigid and
planar and prevents free rotation about it. Rotation is instead possible
around the two single bonds flanking each alpha carbon, and those
rotations are what allow a chain to fold at all.

## aa-05: Primary and secondary structure

Primary structure is simply the sequence of residues, written from the
free amino end to the free carboxyl end. Secondary structure is local,
repeating folding stabilised entirely by hydrogen bonds between backbone
atoms — not side chains. The two common motifs are the alpha helix, a
right-handed coil in which each backbone carbonyl hydrogen bonds to an
amide roughly four residues further along, and the beta pleated sheet, in
which extended strands lie alongside one another and hydrogen bond across
the gap.

## aa-06: Tertiary and quaternary structure

Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional shape of a single
polypeptide, and unlike secondary structure it is driven mainly by side
chain interactions: hydrophobic residues clustering away from water,
hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions between oppositely charged side chains,
and covalent disulfide bridges between cysteine residues. Quaternary
structure exists only in proteins built from more than one polypeptide
chain, and describes how those separate subunits associate. Haemoglobin,
with four subunits, has quaternary structure; myoglobin, a single chain,
does not.

## aa-07: Denaturation

Denaturation is the loss of a protein's higher-order structure while its
primary sequence stays intact — the peptide bonds are not broken, so the
sequence survives even as the shape is destroyed. Heat, extremes of pH,
and certain solutes can all cause it, and because function depends on
shape, a denatured protein generally loses activity. This is a common
exam trap: denaturation does not break the backbone, and describing it as
"breaking the protein into amino acids" confuses it with hydrolysis.

## aa-08: MCAT relevance and common traps

Frequently tested points include recognising that only side chains differ
between amino acids, that the peptide bond's rigidity comes from
resonance rather than from being a true double bond, and that secondary
structure is backbone hydrogen bonding while tertiary structure is side
chain driven. Another recurring trap is assuming every amino acid is
chiral — glycine is not. Students should also be able to reason from side
chain polarity to the likely location of a residue in a folded protein.
